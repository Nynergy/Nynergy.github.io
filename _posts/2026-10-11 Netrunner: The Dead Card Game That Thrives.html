Netrunner is a very special game, and one of my favorite to ever exist. Some people may find it strange that I still play a "dead" card game that officially ended production back in 2018, but those in the know will tell you the game is not only still alive, but thriving in a way it never quite did under the stewardship of Fantasy Flight Games.

I want to talk a bit about Netrunner and its current incarnation under Null Signal Games, but in order to do the game justice, I first need to do the groan-inducing task that all bloggers and essayists ask of their readers; I need to take you along on my personal journey through card games and how I ended up running nets with my friends since 2016.

My Cards Before Netrunner

Like many young nerds growing up in the 2000's, Yu-Gi-Oh caught my eye from an early age. It helped that I had a friend at school who was also interested in it. We played the game casually until around the time XYZ monsters were introduced, avoiding later additions like Link and Pendulum.

I didn't care much for the competitive scene, so the idea of "the meta" wasn't on my mind; I was only just beginning to dip my toes into "proper" deckbuilding around themes, card synergies, win conditions, etc. I remember being especially fond of a Blackwing deck that I built from cards I had collected at the time.

Eventually that friend changed schools, and with his absence my love of Yu-Gi-Oh began to fade. It didn't help that I was being sucked into the world of video games at the same time, Minecraft taking over nearly every waking moment. The cards were shelved, and to this day I have not played another game of Yu-Gi-Oh, though I still have those cards.

It was only a matter of time before the draw of cardboard would call to me again. Toward the end of middle school, another friend got me into Magic: the Gathering. I had known about the game for a long time, but hadn't really given it much thought during my Yu-Gi-Oh years.

Around the time of Oath of the Gatewatch and Shadows Over Innistrad, the two of us got very heavily into the game, and for the first time I found myself following the Standard format scene, Pro Tours, and more. I discovered the world of alternate formats like Commander, and began to gravitate toward those corners of the game where I felt some sense of personal expression in my deckbuilding.

Magic was not a perfect fit for me, though, as the amount of disposable income needed to play in paper was still a turn-off. I gritted my teeth and stuck to mostly XMage for online play at no cost, but eventually found myself becoming more and more burned out with Magic as well.

With the friend who got me into Magic also moving out of my life, it went much the same way as Yu-Gi-Oh, and I found myself perusing my local game store in search of a different game to sate my cardboard curiosity. I stumbled on a cool-looking box on the "free-to-play" shelf of store-copy games. A cyberpunk game called "Netrunner" that promised an "asymmetrical" game (what on earth could that mean?) where players "install" cards onto the board and interact with them in a way that isn't just bashing their dudes against each other.

I came across a couple guys playing the game at a nearby table, asked if I could watch them play, and was instantly hooked. I needed to get my hands on a core set.

Netrunner: From FFG to NSG

I'll spare you the full history and rules of the game, and give you a brief overview instead. Android: Netrunner, as it was coined by Fantasy Flight Games in 2012, is a Living Card Game (LCG) where one player plays as an evil megacorporation in a dystopian cyberpunk future, and another player plays as a legendary hacker, called a "runner". The Corp player wants to advance their sinister agenda by installing certain cards from their hand and scoring them by performing specific actions. These "agenda" cards only exist in the Corp's deck, and the Runner's goal is to install a "rig" of programs, hardware, and other resources that will enable them to hack into the Corp's servers and steal those agendas, taking the points for themselves.

The LCG aspect is one that immediately drew me as someone who was weary from the booster-pack business model of Magic. FFG would release various products containing new cards, and when you bought that product, you would receive a full play set of every card in the set. The current makers of the game, Null Signal Games (NSG), have improved upon this model even further by making all of their products free for print-and-play via PDF files they provide on their site. These printed proxies, as well as all other kinds of proxies, are legal for every level of competitive play, including the World Championship. This made Netrunner a very affordable game for a high school kid without any income -- which is to say, very affordable for his parents!

The asymmetrical aspect of Netrunner, along with the very "tangible" feeling of making high-stakes "runs" against the Corp's defended servers, piqued my interest; the depth of strategy and self-expression involved in deckbuilding sold me even further. The game features different Corp and Runner factions, and cards belonging to a faction will cost a certain amount of "influence" to import into decks of another faction. This type of deckbuilding resource is often scarce, and adds so many interesting decisions to the process of building a deck. I was in love. Throughout 2016 and 2017, I spent my time installing "ice" to protect my servers, running R&D to dig for agendas to steal, and making friends that I still play the game with to this day.

Eventually, I went off to college, and found little to no time for Netrunner. In hindsight, I really wished that I had still tried to make more space for it during my undergrad years. The game came to an official end when FFG didn't renew the license, and stopped production in 2018.

The game did not completely die off, however, as a fan-run collective of volunteers continued making new cards compatible with the original game and holding events. This group eventually settled on the name Null Signal Games, and they still make the game to this day.

When I returned to the game during the COVID-19 Pandemic in 2021, I realized that Netrunner still had plenty of life in it, and I began to foster our local group of players, called a "meta", as it struggled with dwindling numbers.

Over the past few years, our meta has grown from 2 consistent players to about 4 or 5, with 8-10 willing to show up to larger, more infrequent events. According to people in our group who have played since launch in 2012, it's the most consistent numbers we've had since around 2014.

NSG has continued putting out new sets, updating ban lists, running the organized play season, providing prizing, and fostering an incredible community. That community is something I finally got to experience on a larger scale in 2024 when myself and another long-time player went to San Francisco for the Netrunner World Championship. There, I realized just how important the community is to the continued survival and success of the game.

Worlds

Netrunner is friendly, open, and incredibly queer, and I could not be happier to be part of a community that accepts and celebrates those facets of its player base, especially given my time in other, more "notorious" game communities.

Arriving at Worlds in San Francisco I was presented with hundreds of fellow Netrunner players from all kinds of communities, coming together to celebrate this incredible game that we all love. Those same communities, often underrepresented and unheard, are more and more often finding themselves shown and spotlit on cards or in game lore, thanks to the players and volunteers at NSG.

The community kept growing after Worlds 2024, and at Worlds 2026 in Montreal we had the most players ever participate in the main Standard tournament at any NSG organized play event, nearly 300. This "dead" game will never cease to amaze me, and it's all thanks to the people who keep it alive.

At both Worlds events I had opponents gift me play sets of alt art cards they had created, or special tokens they made for the game; I supported individual artists and creators selling their own celebrations of Netrunner at the "Artist Colony" table; I played against people with special accommodations for their games, like those with visual impairments; I played against volunteers who work with NSG, including the head of Organized Play; I participated in player-run side events where you could experience alternate formats of the game and win various prizes; I attended a round table of tournament organizers from across the globe; I laughed and smiled and played the greatest card game of all time against the greatest and kindest strangers I had ever met, and some of them became my friends.

Netrunner is a game made by the players, not a for-profit company. The game is in a better place right now than it was back in the FFG days, not only in terms of player engagement, but also competitive meta, online organized play, and much more. That's not to say FFG was a terrible steward of Netrunner; I just think NSG has absolutely knocked it out of the park, and the nature of how the game is made today, and by who, is a large part of it.

The Future of Netrunner

That sense of community is what continues to propel me forward with the game, even after the novelty of the game systems has worn off. It's that same sense of community that we're now focusing on fostering locally more than ever before.

Now that we've brought in some new players and can maintain higher player counts for local events, we're holding events more frequently. At time of writing, we try to hold events once a month. This could be a casual event, where we just show up and jam games for 5 hours on a Saturday and teach the game to anyone interested in learning, or it could be a small tournament for some prizes.

One thing that I've been running as a sort-of tournament organizer is what we call the "New York Netrunner League". It sounds a bit bigger than it really is; it's just a casual asynchronous online league where players of any and all skill levels are paired up in two-week periods. They have those two weeks to schedule their games, play them online, and report their results. You can play the games in different formats as well, to accommodate the preferences of different players. The scoring system is designed to favor participation rather than winning, as that's the real purpose of the league, to get people in Upstate New York playing more Netrunner, even if they can't make it out to our weekly in-person meetups.

Something that I'm exploring more earnestly recently for our events is the vast world of alternate formats. You have constructed formats like Standard, Startup, and Eternal, Standard follows closely to the format of the same name in Magic. Startup uses an even smaller card pool that focuses on the most recent set, and serves as a great entry point to constructed play for new players. Eternal's card pool contains (almost) every card ever printed, and features a point-system as the main balancing mechanic, rather than a ban list. but there are also limited formats like pack and cube drafts, A group of 4 to 8 players sits down and drafts decks from random packs of cards taken from a custom-made set. You can make a cube based around a theme, a certain set, or whatever else you like. There's an endless wellspring of personal expression in curating the card pools of limited formats like this. "random" formats like Chimera, Chimera is a very fun format, but a difficult one to put together for paper play. Effectively, you and your opponent are both given random decks that could be filled with any cards from across the game's history. The time and effort it takes to sleeve and unsleeve all those cards, randomize the decks while maintaining a certain level of economy and other staple cards, all of it grows exponentially as you add more players to the mix. A small group of players currently maintain the ban list, but you are free to make your own ban list when you run your own event. or "alternate meta" formats like Pawnshop. Pawnshop is a dumpster diver's dream format. It effectively uses the same card pool as Eternal, but half the cards are missing -- specifically, the good ones. There's a fan-created website called Trash-or-Busto that creates a ranking of the strongest cards across all of Netrunner. Pawnshop only allows players to use cards from the bottom 50% of those rankings, leading to all manner of jank, low-power decks that make players like me grin from ear to ear. I want our group to dabble in these formats more often for events, so we've put together some ideas on incorporating Pawnshop into our league, and I've also been working on updating a small cube of old FFG cards that we could draft together.

Netrunner is a dead game that continues to thrive. If any part of this diatribe about cardboard and queer communities interested you, I would highly recommend giving Netrunner a try. You can find everything you need to get started (for totally free!) on NSG's site. We would love to have you be part of our Netrunner community.

I hope to see you out there next time I make it to Worlds. Even better if I can sit down across from you and play a game or two. Remember: Always Be Running!

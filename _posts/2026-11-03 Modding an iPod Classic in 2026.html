A little while ago I re-discovered my parents' old 5th generation iPod from 2005 when I was going through some old tech that was stored away. I was surprised that it still worked, and had fun listening to a snapshot of my parents' taste in music during the 2000's. Unsurprisingly, my father and I shared a lot of the same taste; some Brian Eno, Radiohead, Chris Squire, and plenty more. My mother had some good picks, too; Billy Joel and Genesis especially.

Up until now I've been running my own music server on my homelab, and streaming to my phone when I needed a mobile listening solution. But after playing around with the iPod a bit and looking into the ongoing modding scene for classic iPods, I realized that it might actually be the solution to a few different problems I was facing.

1. Offline Backup I needed a backup of my music collection outside of my homelab.
2. Offline Listening I wanted access to my music without an internet connection.
3. Reusing Old Tech I wanted to find a use for old technology instead of disposing of it.

So I set out to order some parts, make some mods to it, and make it my own. This blog post isn't really a how-to for modding an iPod; for that, I would direct you to this wonderful site that I found incredibly helpful during the whole process.

Before

This iPod is a classic 5th generation model, also called an iPod Video. Specifically, it was the thick backplate version, and all the parts were still in pretty good shape. However, some of the innards would need to be upgraded, and some of the "outtards" could use a touch-up as well.

The 60 GB hard drive (yes, a spinning disk hard drive from 2005) was not going to cut it for me, so I opted to buy an iFlash Quad adapter that would let me use some micro SD cards for more reliable storage, and a lot more of it.

Battery life wasn't a huge concern for me, but if I was gonna go so far as to open up the iPod and swap some of its guts, I decided I might as well increase the battery capacity as well. Because this is a thick backplate model, I went with a 3000mAh battery that would fit snugly inside.

Finally, while the faceplate was still in decent condition, I wanted to use something with slightly less scratches and marks over the screen. I liked the black, but I wanted something with more color, so I bought a bright yellow faceplate and clickwheel, though I ended up keeping the black clickwheel.

All of these parts came from different sellers, and often different countries, so they arrived piecemeal across a few months. This gave me the opportunity to modify the iPod one part at a time, and granted me a good understanding of how all the parts are connected on the inside.

Storage

First to arrive was the iFlash adapter. I very carefully opened the iPod by following some video instructions, disconnected the old hard drive, and connected the iFlash adapter in its place. At the moment I'm using two 256 GB micro SD cards for a total of 512 GB of storage, but in the future I can always upgrade to 1 TB if necessary.

Once the SD cards were all set, I closed the iPod back up, re-installed iPod OS on it with iTunes, and then installed Rockbox OS alongside that. Rockbox is my preferred OS for this device, as I can easily add music on Linux without the need for iTunes, customize the interface with themes, and much more.

I also took this opportunity to replace my old bulky wallet with a smaller wallet case that can fit the iPod, which you can see below. Now I can carry the iPod with me without feeling like I have way too much stuff in my pockets.

Yellow

The faceplate and battery both arrived pretty close together, so I installed them at the same time. Quite a lot of yellow, and I like it a lot. It was at this time that I decided I wanted to keep the original black clickwheel, as it gave the iPod a kind of "sunflower" vibe with the black and yellow.

I also kept the backplate, because it has some sentimental value. I mentioned this iPod originally belonged to my parents; this is because it was a 10-year wedding anniversary gift to them, and the backplate features an inscription celebrating that anniversary. I think it's very nice to keep that around, so I decided not to replace it.

The battery didn't quite fit the way I had expected it to, but because I stuck some padding on top of the iFlash adapter when I installed it, I was able to add the battery in a place where it won't rattle around and cause trouble inside the iPod.

Once everything was reassembled, the mods were done! I christened my new (old) iPod with a new (old) name: Terpsichore.

After

My music server on my homelab is called Muse, and the squeezelite client that I have hooked up to my hi-fi system for streaming off it is called Siren, so it only felt right to name her after one of the muses.

Terpsichore has a bright yellow face, houses an offline backup of my music collection, and joins her sisters/daughters in my music ecosystem. I've been using her almost entirely in place of my phone for listening while out of the house, especially at work and in my car.

Not only was is fun to mod the iPod, but it's been an absolute joy using it as well. It's been a little over a month since I finished the mods, and I haven't really run into anything I could complain about. It's almost like they used to make good products that encouraged good listening patterns and lifestyle choices, before capitalism and enshittification did what they always do.

A big reason I did this was because it solved some very specific problems for me, and I already had the iPod sitting around in my house. I don't know if I would recommend someone else do this if they don't have an old iPod already, because at time of writing they can be quite an investment. But, if you can get your hands on one for relatively cheap, I would highly recommend you mod it. Personalize it, make it yours, and continue to take power out of the hands of streaming platforms and other bad actors of the music industry.
